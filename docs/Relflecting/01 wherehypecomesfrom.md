## Drowning, or delivering, wuthin constraints?

### Through a Computer Science lens ...

I was talking top a recent Computer Science graduate and we were comparing our study experiences. Thinking through the last 50 years of computing history, I drew a conclusion that in my day, Computer Science teaching was largely about ways of overcoming the constraints imposed by the technology of the day ... 16-bit CPU / 64k RAM anyone *(and that's the larger departmental computing stuff)*?. It's not wonder that we didn't have a lot of complex data structures, or implicit language data structures like Pythomn's dictionaries. Maybe the years have filtered some of memories I recall a lot of time spent studying *mathematical methods* a.k.a. 'ways to approximate big maths problems'.

Over the years we have had Moore's Law to thank for creating large compute architecture, which in turn have created spec to develop the technology infrastructures we rely on today: network access, graphical computing, databases, and the emergent property we don't like but have to not only endure, but by necessity respond to, cyber-security. Access to all of these technology foundations, made ubiquitous by open-source, leads to a modern computer science degree being far more about exploitation of an abundance of capability.

#### Databases Then
An example for me of the differences between then and now is that my database course win spring of 1982 was taught from one of the very few textbooks available at thme that covered the new-fangled art of normalised data in tables. It's fair to say that state of the art at that time was largely in [ISAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISAM) data stores, and any access to real code for table-based data was in the new, and very expensive, products from IBM or Oracle. Much of the course, and the exams, was  based on deducing the logic of data normalisation.

#### Contemporary Database Learning
To be honest, I've not experienced any modern database teaching, but I receive frequent course details for commerical training, I see YouTube training and guides, and even access videos of University lectures on the topic.

If I were asked to teach *"databases"* I could cover programming in SQL; how to design and manage a plethora of available database products; or even comparative database technologies along the limes of [Seven Databases in Seven Weeks](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Seven-Databases-Weeks-2e-dp-1680502530/dp/1680502530/ref=dp_ob_title_bk). I suspect any university modules face a similar dilemma.

### Technology Advances = New Freedoms?
In sharing my views of how periods of technology hype come about, let me air my view that it is rarely a new technology alone that drives a period of hype. In my experience, the factors that that create a hype cycle include:

- A upgrade or development of an existing technology that 
- lifts a pre-existing constraint and combines with
- some novel circumstances that
- in combination serve the zeitgeist of that time, and then
- is presented with a new name

I agree with this [recent keynote speech by Richard Campbell](https://youtu.be/uWnUnMphmPM?si=F_dtVJ1WZYUdbsfF) that a key to hype cycles is that the factors in hype are not well understood, or to precis Campbell: "When people can be sold anything because they don't know better"

To summarise here - ___The technology alone doesn't drive a hype-cycle. Instead a combination of factors occurring at the same time drives a hype-cycle___

### "We've been here before"
Looking back at my university days, I sometimes say that we could have created what is in a mobile phone at the time ... it would just have been the size of two large lorries or more[^1], probably required a direct connection to an electricity supply to serve all of the power needs, and have needed the efforts of avery available programmer in the world at that time (_probably not in truth, just an awful lot of programmers_). When us older folks look at something  being presented as new and say _"That's not new, we've had that for a while"_ we'rer referring to the fact that the various component parts really are only developments of what has been before. 






[^1]: I base this estimate on the basis that my first programming role after university required a rack of equipment that was performing teh task of a delayed single silicon chip
